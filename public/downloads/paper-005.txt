Abstract

Every dominant interpretability method — sparse autoencoders, linear probes, the logit lens,
activation patching — answers one question: what directions live in the activations? This framing has no ground truth, depends on arbitrary choices (dictionary size, sparsity penalty, layer),
and produces features that are unstable and that quietly swallow one another. I argue the
framing itself is the error. A trained autoregressive network is not a bag of directions; it is a
process, and a stochastic process has a mathematically forced, choice-free notion of its own
computational units: the causal states of computational mechanics, which a classical theorem
certifies as the unique minimal sufficient statistic for prediction. I develop Causal-State
Interpretability (CSI): define a feature not as a direction in activation space but as a causal
equivalence class of internal states — two states are the same feature iff the model's entire
predictive future is identical. Around this object I assemble four layers of structure: an information geometry (the belief manifold with its Fisher–Rao metric), a spectral / Koopman theory
of the model's internal dynamics whose eigenmodes are the natural features, and a renormalization-group flow over the prediction horizon that separates the computation that matters from
the noise. The theory makes two falsifiable, laptop- scale predictions: (i) a sparse-autoencoder
feature is absorbed exactly when it tries to split a single causal state — non-causal directions
are spurious by construction; and (ii) grokking is a phase transition in causal-state count, visible in the spectrum before it is visible in the loss. I ground the program with a real experiment: a small recurrent model trained on a partially observed Markov process reaches the

§16 Objections and responses
The strongest version of a program is the one that has met its own best objections in
writing.
"Causal states are intractable for a real language model." The exact infinite-future object can be; the usable object is the horizon- , tolerance- approximation

(§10, App

B), and crucially we estimate it by querying the model's own predictions, not by sample statistics of the data — a far easier problem than classical -machine reconstruction. The relevant question is not "can we get

exactly" but "does

converge usefully as

grows,"

which §14.1's convergence test answers empirically.
"This is just probing for the belief state, which is already known." The belief-state finding is the empirical seed, and I say so plainly. But CSI is not the observation that belief
geometry is represented; it is the use of the causal state as the definition of a feature, from
which follow the causal criterion and absorption theorem (§11), the Koopman feature basis
and its stability prediction (§9), the horizon-RG account of scaling and emergence (§10,
§12), and a unification of metric, dynamics, and scale. None of these is "probe for the HMM
state."
"Real models are sub-optimal and real data isn't a clean process." True, and the theory
degrades gracefully rather than breaking. Sub-optimality makes

only approximately in‐

jective and blurs the causal/gauge split into a spectrum — but the criterion (causal effect

on the future) is defined for any model, optimal or not, and the §11 protocol runs unchanged. The clean theorems describe the attractor the model is moving toward; the program measures how close it is.
"The linear representation hypothesis works fine." It works until it produces absorption,
polysemantic atoms, and seed-unstable dictionaries — the exact failures §9 and §11 derive
from first principles. CSI does not deny that features are often approximately linear (the
embedding

is often near-linear on

); it denies that linearity is the definition, and it pre‐

dicts precisely the cases where linear descriptions fracture.
"Why must the model represent causal states at all?" Because an optimal predictor's
state is a sufficient statistic (§2), and the causal state is the minimal one (Thm 4.1); a model that failed to encode it would be sub-optimal on some past. This is not an assumption
about architecture — it is forced by the loss. The experiment of §13 confirms it at toy scale:
optimality and an

belief representation arrived together.

"Measuring causal effect requires interventions you can't trust." The intervention is the
same activation edit the field already performs in patching and steering; CSI's only addition
is to read its output as the reality score of a feature. If one distrusts interventional measurement, one distrusts the whole causal-interpretability program, not CSI specifically.
