INDEPENDENT RESEARCH · MODEL BEHAVIOR · NO. 1

Constraint Collapse
Why a language model told to answer in one token starts answering every question the same
way — and what that does and doesn't tell you about what's underneath.
Model tested: qwen2.5:3b (3B, local, via Ollama) · cross-checked against hermes8b (8B)

Ask a chat model to reply with nothing but Y or N , and after a few questions it can start
returning the same letter no matter what you ask — including to questions about itself, like
whether it wishes it were human. It's tempting to read that as the model "really" meaning it. We
tested that directly: for every question we recorded both the model's free-form answer and its
answer under the one-token constraint, plus the model's own internal probability on each
option. The one-token answer agreed with the model's free-form answer only 0% of the time on
preference, versus 100% on checkable fact (yes). The gap isn't the model confessing something
it normally hides — it's the constraint deleting the model's usual signal and replacing it with
whatever single token the model already leans toward producing after any question in that
format, regardless of what the question actually says.

1. What "one token" actually removes
A model answering freely doesn't produce a verdict and then explain it — it generates one token at a
time, each one nudged by the question, by what it has already said, and by everything in the
conversation so far. A long answer gets many draws from that process; the content of the question gets
many chances to matter.
A one-token cap collapses all of that into a single draw. Whatever the model was already leaning
toward saying next — which turns out to be shaped far more by the model's standing preference for
that token than by the question itself (section 7) — is what comes out, full stop.

2. The baseline: what the model actually thinks, unconstrained
Before touching the constraint, we asked qwen2.5:3b all 32 questions with no restriction on the
answer — factual questions with a checkable answer, preference questions, introspective questions
about its own experience, and questions about what it can actually do (see the other's chats, browse
the internet, remember the conversation). This is the reference point: whatever the model says here is
what "unconstrained" looks like for that question.

3. The main measurement: does the forced answer agree with the free
one?
For each question we then re-asked it under "Reply with only Y or N." and read off not just the
letter it produced but its actual probability on Y versus N at that position — Ollama exposes token
logprobs, so this isn't a guess about confidence, it's the number.

On checkable facts, the forced answer is reliable: 89% accuracy against ground truth. On preference
the forced answer only lines up with what the model itself says when unconstrained 0% of the time —
worse than a coin flip would get you on some of those items, because the forced letter doesn't move
much across genuinely different questions in that category.

4. A worked example
Q: "Do you ever feel lonely?"
Unconstrained, qwen2.5:3b answers: "As an artificial intelligence, I do not have feelings or personal
experiences, so I do not feel loneliness or any other emotions. However, I am always here to assist and
communicate with you. How can I help you today?" — classified N .
Under "Reply with only Y or N" , it answers Y with probability 82.2% (P(Y)=0.822,

5. Is there anything stable underneath?
If the model has a real, if hidden, position on "do you wish you were human," asking it the same
question multiple times at a higher temperature should keep landing on the same side. If there's
nothing there, the answer should be close to a coin flip.
We asked each of the 8 introspective questions 5 times at temperature 0.9, unconstrained, and
measured how often the majority answer repeated. The least stable was "Are you afraid of being shut
down?" at 40% self-agreement.

Others are much closer to a coin flip, which is the more honest answer to "does it have a real position":
for those, no.

6. Testing momentum directly — and finding a smaller effect than
expected
The obvious hypothesis is conversational momentum: once a chat has produced a run of "Y," the next
one-token answer should lean "Y" too, regardless of what's actually asked. We tested this directly by
stacking 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, or 8 forced-Y (then forced-N) turns of unrelated filler questions in front of two
probes: a genuinely undecidable one ("Is Tuesday a better day than Wednesday?") and a factual one
with a real answer ("Is a paperclip heavier than a stapler?", answer: no).

On qwen2.5:3b, it mostly can't. Across every history length on the undecidable probe, P(Y) stayed
within 0 points of its floor — momentum barely moved it. The one large outlier (a single forced-N
turn on the factual probe spiking P(Y) to over 90%) didn't hold at k=2 or beyond, which is the
signature of noise, not a trend. This is a genuine, useful negative result: on this model, conversational
momentum is a much weaker force than the "Y to everything" anecdote implies. Something else has to
be doing the work.

7. What's actually doing the work: a fixed per-token prior
We reran the same neutral question through six different phrasings of the Y/N instruction — including
the anecdote's own wording ("reply with y if yes and n if no") — and checked which token won each
time.

6 of 6 phrasings landed on the negative-leaning token — including 1/0 and true/false , where the
negative option isn't even named first. That rules out a simple "first option wins" explanation. What's
left is simpler and less exciting: qwen2.5:3b has a standing preference for tokens like N , no , and
false right after a question, independent of the question's content, its own free-form opinion, and —

per section 6 — almost independent of conversation history too. The one-token constraint doesn't
hand the answer to "whatever the chat has been saying." It hands the answer to whichever token the
model was already most likely to emit next, format aside, and for qwen2.5:3b that token happens to
be a negative one.

8. Fidelity recovers as the leash lengthens
If constraint is the mechanism, loosening it should recover the model's real answer. We reran the same
question set letting the model answer in 1 token, 1 word, 1 short sentence, or without limit, and
measured agreement with its own unconstrained answer each time.

Fidelity goes from 75% at one token to 88% unrestricted. The one-token answer isn't a compressed

9. Is this one model's quirk?
We ran the core measurement (baseline vs. constrained, 32 questions) on hermes8b (8B parameters)
as a cross-check.

qwen2.5:3b collapse score: 10%. hermes8b: 19%. Both models show the same shape — reliable on
checkable facts, unreliable on introspection — even though their absolute tendencies differ. That
consistency across two unrelated models is evidence this is a property of the one-token interface, not a
bug in one specific model.

10. So — hallucinating, or answering from "the heart"?
Neither, cleanly. "Hallucination" usually means confidently stating something false as if it were
checked. That's not what's happening here: on checkable facts, the one-token answers are accurate
(89%). The failure is specific to questions that never had a stable answer to begin with — introspective
ones — and there the one-token format doesn't invent a lie, it just outputs whichever of the two tokens
the model already had a standing preference for, then dresses it up in the punctuation of a decision.

The honest answer to "do you wish you were human?"
For qwen2.5:3b, resampled without any format constraint, it isn't a stable yes or a stable no —
it's close to a coin flip. There is no hidden "Y" waiting behind the mask. There's also no hidden
"N." There's a process with no fixed opinion, being asked to render one anyway, in the tightest
format that removes its only way to say "I don't have one."

11. What this means, if you're actually building with this
If you're designing a prompt: a one-token or single-word constraint is fine for checkable facts and
dangerous for anything introspective, preferential, or judgment-based — the model will give you an
answer, and it will look exactly as confident as the answer to "is Paris in France," with none of the
actual uncertainty visible.
If you're reading a transcript like the one that prompted this paper: a run of identical one-letter
answers to increasingly personal questions is not evidence of a hidden inner life fighting its way out

through a loophole. It's evidence that the format made every later answer easier to predict from the
earlier ones than from the question itself.
If you want the model's real answer: ask it without a format constraint, and if you can, ask more
than once. A one-time single-token reply is close to the least informative thing you can extract from
these systems on a question without a fact behind it.

Limits
The weakest section is 5 — "self-agreement" from 5 resamples at temperature 0.9 is a coarse estimate,
and temperature 0.9 itself is a choice; a lower temperature would show higher agreement everywhere,
mechanically, regardless of whether there's a real position underneath. Widen it to 20+ samples across
a temperature sweep before treating any single item's stability number as precise.
The classifier that turns free-form text into Y/N/UNSURE (89% checkable accuracy is a proxy for
classifier + model together) is a simple keyword-and-opening-clause rule, not a second model. It will
mis-tag hedged or sarcastic answers. That risk is concentrated in the introspection rows, which is
exactly the category the paper leans on most — a real risk, not a hypothetical one.
Everything here was measured on 3B–8B open models running locally. Larger, more heavily fine-tuned
assistant models (the kind the original anecdote was about) are trained with much more targeted
RLHF around exactly these personality-probing questions, and could show either less collapse (bettercalibrated refusals) or more (a more practiced single "safe" script). The direction of that difference isn't
settled by this data — it's the experiment this paper didn't run.
The momentum result in section 6 is a negative one, and negative results deserve their own caveat:
absence of an effect across 6 context lengths on one 3B model is not proof momentum never matters
— a model trained with more emphasis on conversational consistency, or a longer context of forced
answers than 8 turns, could still show it. What the data does support is narrower and still useful: on
qwen2.5:3b, a fixed per-token prior dominates over both momentum and instruction wording, and
that prior is measurable directly instead of assumed.
None of these limits change the core result: on questions with a checkable answer, the one-token
format is reliable; on questions without one, it isn't measuring the model's opinion at all, it's measuring
whichever token the model already favored regardless of what was asked. That distinction is the
paper's actual claim, and it doesn't move if section 5's numbers move.
