Autocorrection Fidelity
When a model fixes your typo, it doesn't fail by giving up. It fails by confidently handing you a
different, correctly-spelled word — and the fix isn't a smarter speller, it's a sentence around the
word.
Model tested: qwen2.5:3b (3B, local, via Ollama) · 15 words × 4 corruption types × 2 severities

We corrupted 15 common English words with realistic typos — deletions, insertions,
transpositions, and adjacent-key substitutions — at two severities, then asked qwen2.5:3b to
recover the intended word two ways: the corrupted word alone, and the same word sitting
inside an ordinary sentence. At light corruption, asking about the word alone works better than
sentence context (90% vs 73%) — context is overhead the model doesn't need yet. At heavy
corruption the lines cross: alone, recovery drops to 55%, while sentence context climbs to 65%.
Context isn't a nice-to-have. It's the thing that's actually doing the correcting once the word
itself stops being recoverable on its own.

1. Two ways to ask for a correction
Autocorrect can fail two different ways. It can say "I don't know what you meant" — safe, if unhelpful.
Or it can quietly substitute a different word entirely, spelled perfectly, and move on. The second failure
is the dangerous one, because nothing about the output signals that anything went wrong.

2. The setup
15 words that are commonly misspelled in real writing ( receive , definitely , necessary ,
rhythm , and 11 others), each corrupted four ways — a letter deleted, a letter inserted next to a real

keyboard neighbor, two adjacent letters swapped, or a letter replaced by a keyboard neighbor — at
one and two edits. For each of the resulting 120 corrupted words, we asked qwen2.5:3b to recover the
original twice: once with just the corrupted word, once with the same corruption embedded in a
natural sentence built for that word. Recovery counts as a hit if the target word appears in the model's
reply.

3. The main measurement: a crossover, not a straight line
The naive expectation is that context should always help. It doesn't — it depends entirely on how
badly the word is damaged.

At one edit, the word is usually still recognizable on its own — asking in isolation gets 90% right,
while the sentence version actually does slightly worse (73%), because "find the one misspelled word
AND fix it" is a harder combined task than "fix this word" when the word alone was already enough. At
two edits, isolation drops to 55% and the sentence version pulls ahead to 65%. Context is not a
uniform improvement. It's a rescue that only pays for itself once the word has stopped carrying enough
information by itself.

4. Which kind of typo is worst
Not all two-edit corruptions are equally damaging. Deleting or inserting letters tends to leave a word
looking obviously broken — a shape nothing else matches. Replacing letters with keyboard-adjacent
ones is different: it can accidentally land on a completely different valid word.

Two-letter substitution is the outlier: standalone recovery falls to 20%, worse than every other

6. The failure mode is confident substitution, not refusal
Across all 33 standalone misses in the whole experiment, we checked whether the model's wrong
answer was itself a plausible, correctly-spelled English word, or visible garbage.

32 of 33 misses (97%) were clean, correctly spelled, entirely different words — "government" became
"ghostbuster," "schedule" became "suggestion," "privilege" became "prefecture." None of these look like
failures at a glance. That's the actual risk: a spelling corrector that's wrong and confident-looking is
harder to catch than one that's wrong and obviously broken.

7. What this means, if you're actually building with this
If you're building a "fix this word" feature in isolation — a search-box did-you-mean, a username
field, a single-field form — know that your worst-case failure isn't "no suggestion," it's a different,
unrelated, perfectly spelled word with no signal attached. Consider showing the original alongside any
correction, especially past one or two edits of damage.
If you're building anything that already has surrounding text — a document, a chat message, a
form with other filled fields — don't correct the isolated word. Feed the model the sentence. The
rescue effect in section 3 is free: it costs nothing but sending the context you probably already have.
If you're evaluating a spellchecker or an LLM-based one, testing only on isolated words will make it
look worse than it will perform in its real, in-context use, and testing only on light corruption will hide
the substitution failure mode entirely — it only shows up at two-plus edits.

Limits
The weakest section is 6. "Correctly spelled English word" is a cheap regex check ( [a-z]+ , length ≥
3), not a real dictionary or semantic check — a nonsense string that happens to be all letters would
count as a "real word" false positive here. Spot-checking the examples in section 5 and 6 shows
genuine English words, not artifacts of the check, but a dictionary pass would make this section's
number precise instead of approximate.
15 words and 2 severities is a small grid — enough to show the crossover exists and roughly where it
sits, not enough to pin down the exact edit-distance threshold where context starts winning. Section

4's per-kind breakdown (15 words per cell) is the noisiest set of numbers in the paper for the same
reason.
The sentence templates were written by hand, one per word, and are unavoidably easy in one respect:
the target word is usually the most content-bearing word in its sentence, so context has more to work
with than it might in messier real writing (run-on sentences, multiple typos, unfamiliar topics). The
rescue effect measured here is plausibly an upper bound on what context alone buys you.
Everything was measured on one 3B model. Section 4's specific ranking of which corruption type is
worst could easily be different on a model with a different tokenizer or training mix — the tested
claim that survives a model change is the shape in section 3 (context helps more as damage increases),
not the specific percentages attached to it.
