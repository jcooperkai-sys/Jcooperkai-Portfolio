INDEPENDENT RESEARCH · TOKENIZATION · NO. 2

Cheap Damage

Token cost is a bad proxy for information loss
Jeffery Eckert · Independent · 2026

Character-level corruption is usually treated as one phenomenon: insertions, deletions and
swaps get bundled together as "noise" and applied interchangeably. This paper separates two of
them and finds they behave in opposite directions. At four edits, insertion inflates token count
by 400% but the original word is still recoverable 87.5% of the time from the corrupted string
alone. Deletion inflates tokens by only 146% — and recovery collapses to 8.3%. The cheaper
corruption is the far more destructive one. Context repairs the gap almost entirely for deletion
(+54.2 points) and does nothing at all for insertion. 384 trials, 24 words, qwen2.5:3b.

1. The assumption being tested
Work on text robustness generally treats character corruption as a single axis with a magnitude.
Benchmarks apply insertions, deletions and swaps as interchangeable noise, and edit distance is used
as the measure of how badly something has been damaged.
That framing contains an assumption worth checking: that two corruptions of the same edit distance
do comparable damage. They do not, and the reason is visible before any measurement.

Figure 1. Both corruptions are edit distance 4 from the original. Insertion keeps every original letter and adds
padding. Deletion removes letters permanently.
Insert a character and nothing is lost — the original letters are all still present, just diluted. Delete one
and that information is gone from the string for good. Edit distance counts operations. It does not
count what was destroyed.

2. Insertion costs far more in tokens
The first measurement is the one that looks intuitive, and it points the wrong way.

Figure 2. Token inflation over the clean word. Insertion is consistently and increasingly more expensive.
Insertion climbs steeply — 196% at one edit, 400% at four. Deletion stays roughly flat around 146%.
Mechanically this makes sense. Inserting a character creates a letter pair that has not appeared in that
position, so the tokenizer's greedy match breaks early and everything after is misaligned — and each
new insertion breaks it again. Deletion shortens the word, and a shortened word is often still a valid
prefix the matcher can consume in one piece.
If token count tracked damage, insertion would be the corruption to worry about.

3. It does not track damage
The same corrupted words, handed to a model with no surrounding sentence, with one question: what
was the original word?

Figure 3. Recovery from the corrupted string alone. The expensive corruption survives; the cheap one does
not.
At four edits, insertion recovers 87.5% of the time and deletion recovers 8.3%.
Insertion costs 2.7× more tokens and is roughly 11× more survivable. The relationship between
cost and damage is not weak — it is inverted.

Insertion barely degrades at all: 95.8% at one edit, 87.5% at four, having quadrupled in token cost
along the way. Deletion falls off a cliff — 95.8%, 58.3%, 33.3%, 8.3%.
The explanation is the one from Figure 1. ssufffficient still contains every letter of sufficient
in order; reading it is a filtering problem. sufint does not contain them, and no amount of attention
to the string can recover characters that are not in it.

4. What context is actually worth
Repeating everything with the surrounding sentence restored isolates what context contributes,
because nothing else differs between the two runs.

Figure 4. Recovery with the sentence minus recovery from the word alone. Context is worth almost nothing
for insertion and almost everything for deletion.
Context is worth +54.2 points for deletion at four edits, and +0.0 for insertion.
It only helps when information is genuinely missing.

The deletion column rises monotonically with damage — +4.2, +37.5, +41.7, +54.2 points. The
worse the destruction, the more of the answer comes from the sentence rather than the word.
The insertion column sits at zero throughout and dips slightly negative in the middle, which is noise at
this sample size. That flatness is the point: when the letters are all still present there is nothing for
context to supply, so it supplies nothing.
This gives a cleaner statement of something usually asserted loosely. Language models are described as
robust to typos "because context helps." That is only half right. Context is not a general-purpose
repair mechanism — it is a substitute for missing information, and it does nothing when nothing
is missing.

6. What this changes
Edit distance is not a damage metric. Two corruptions four edits from the original differ by an order
of magnitude in recoverability. Robustness benchmarks that sample corruption types uniformly and
report a single noise level are averaging over an effect larger than most of the differences they are
trying to detect.
Token inflation is not a damage metric either, and it is worse than useless — it points the wrong
way. Any quality or anomaly filter using token count as a corruption signal will flag harmless padding
while passing the corruption that actually destroys meaning.
For adversarial work, deletion is the efficient attack. It is cheap in tokens, hard to detect by
inflation, and far more destructive per edit. An attacker optimising for damage per token should
delete.
For OCR, ASR and transcription pipelines, dropped characters deserve more alarm than
duplicated ones. They are currently treated as comparable error classes; on this evidence they are not
close.

7. Limits
One model (qwen2.5:3b, 3B parameters) and 24 words. The effect sizes are large — 87.5% versus
8.3% is not a marginal difference — but whether the gap narrows in larger models is untested and
worth testing. A bigger model with stronger priors might rescue deletions a small one cannot.
Corruption is synthetic and uniformly positioned. Real deletions cluster differently: dropped characters
in human typing favour doubled letters and unstressed syllables, which may be more recoverable than
random positions. That would make this an upper bound on deletion damage in the wild.
Recovery is scored as exact match to the original word. A model answering with a reasonable different
word counts as failure, which is correct for this question but means the numbers are not a measure of
comprehension in general.

The mechanism in §3 is argued from what the strings contain rather than demonstrated inside the
model. The word-recovery literature has shown models internally rebuild corrupted words; whether
that rebuilding fails for deletion specifically, or merely has nothing to work with, is not something this
experiment can distinguish.
None of these change the direction of the result. The inversion between cost and damage is too large
