INDEPENDENT RESEARCH · TOKENIZATION · NO. 1

The Token Tax

What it costs when text stops looking normal
Jeffery Eckert · Independent · 2026

Language models do not read letters. They read tokens — chunks of text pulled from a fixed list
of about 100,000 known pieces. Text that matches those pieces is cheap. Text that does not
match gets shattered into fragments, and every fragment costs money and makes the model
work harder. This paper measures that cost. Across 2,000 dictionary words, a single doubled
letter inflates token count by 30.9%. The same sentence in Hindi costs 4.0× what it costs in
English. All code and data are included so every number here can be re-run.

1. What a token actually is
Start with the thing everyone gets wrong: a model does not process your text one letter at a time, and
it does not process it one word at a time either. It uses a list of pieces built in advance by looking at
enormous amounts of text and finding which chunks appear most often.
Common whole words earn their own entry. Rare ones do not, so they get assembled from smaller
pieces. The rule is simple and greedy: take the longest known piece that fits, then start again on
whatever is left.

Figure 1. The whole mechanism. A word on the list becomes one number. Break the word and it has to be
rebuilt from scraps.
That is the entire idea. Everything measured below follows from it.

5. Not all text is equal
Typos are one kind of deviation. There are many, and they are not equally expensive. Measuring
characters-per-token — how much text fits in one token — gives a single comparable number.

Figure 5. Characters packed into each token, by kind of text. Higher is cheaper.
Plain English manages 4.77 characters per token. Emoji manage 0.35 — they cost roughly three tokens
each. l33tspeak (1.68) is nearly as expensive as random letters (1.82), because swapping e for 3
destroys exactly the patterns the tokenizer was built on.
Two results are worth pausing on. ALL CAPS costs 46% more than the same sentence in lowercase
— the uppercase forms are separate, rarer entries. And removing every space is cheaper than shouting,
because the tokenizer can still find familiar chunks inside the run-together text.

6. Why adding beats removing
The asymmetry in §3 has a mechanical cause, and the cascade figure shows it.
When you drop a letter, what remains is often still a valid prefix. The greedy rule matches a long piece
and moves on with little damage. When you insert a letter, you create a letter pair that has never
appeared in training in that position — and the greedy match is forced to stop early, right at the
intrusion. It then has to restart mid-word, where it is no longer aligned to any familiar boundary.
Removal shortens a known path. Insertion breaks it, and everything after the break is misaligned. That
is why micchael splits as mic | ch | ael — the split lands exactly on the doubled letter.

7. The part that is not about typos
Everything so far is a self-inflicted cost. This section is not.
The same sentence, professionally translated, costs wildly different amounts depending on the
language it is written in.

Figure 6. One sentence, sixteen languages, token cost for each.
Hindi costs 4.0× what English costs. Greek costs 3.5×. Thai costs 2.83×.
The same meaning. The same sentence. Nobody made a mistake.

APIs bill per token, context windows are measured in tokens, and models attend over tokens. So a
Hindi speaker pays about four times as much for the same request, fits a quarter as much into the
same context window, and hands the model a sentence shattered into four times the pieces.

The cause is not linguistic complexity. It is that the training text used to build the token list was
overwhelmingly English, so English got the efficient entries and everything else got the leftovers. The
languages clustered near English — Spanish, French, German, Portuguese — are the ones that share
its alphabet and appear heavily on the English-language internet.

8. What this means
If you are paying for tokens. Clean up input text before sending it. Not for tidiness — for the 30.9%.
If your model behaves worse on messy input. That is not the model being fragile about spelling. The
word genuinely arrives in pieces. Jackson is one token; jecksen is je + ck + sen , three
fragments that individually mean nothing and must be reassembled before any reasoning can happen.
If you are building for non-English users. Your per-request cost is not a constant. Budget it per
language, and know that context limits bind far tighter for some users than others.

9. If the word is destroyed, why is the meaning not?
This section exists because of an accident. While preparing this paper the author typed:
"push it to github prib"

meaning priv, short for private. It was understood correctly, immediately, with no clarification. On
QWERTY, b sits directly beside v — so this is precisely the adjacent-key typo of §3, the class that
costs +21.2% in tokens.
The tax was paid. The meaning arrived anyway. That is worth explaining, because it is the part
everyone assumes and nobody measures.

The spelling gave almost nothing
Start with what was actually destroyed. prib tokenises as p + rib — and note what the second
fragment is. Not a hint toward the intended word. The unrelated English word rib.
The surviving first fragment is p , which is shared by 10.38% of the dictionary. Compare that to the
misspellings in §5, where the first fragment stayed informative — mikeal keeps mike , narrowing to
0.0% of the vocabulary and pointing almost exactly at the answer.
Worse, the intended word was not a candidate under any spelling rule. Searching every real English
word one keystroke away from prib returns exactly two: prig , prob . Neither is right. priv is an
abbreviation, not a dictionary word, so no amount of spelling correction would ever have produced it.

Spell-checking could not have solved this.
The correct answer was not in the candidate set, the surviving fragment constrained almost
nothing, and the discarded fragment pointed at an unrelated word. Everything the letters offered
was weak or wrong.

The context gave everything

Figure 7. Two independent sources of evidence. The letters were nearly useless here; the context was
decisive.
A repository is pushed as public or private. That is the entire option space, and it comes from the
surrounding words rather than from the mangled one. Two candidates, one of which begins p-r . The
question was already answered before the misspelled word was examined at all.

What a language model is actually doing
It is worth being precise, because the intuitive story is wrong. A model does not detect a typo, look up
a correction, and substitute it. There is no spell checker anywhere in the process.
What it does is the only thing it ever does: predict what should come next given everything so far. The
fragments p and rib arrive as two positions in a sequence that already contains push, it, to, github.
Attention lets every position be interpreted in light of every other, so those two fragments are never
read in isolation — they are read as "whatever belongs here, given a GitHub push, that starts with p."

Only one thing satisfies that. The word is not corrected; it is reconstructed, and the reconstruction
was mostly complete before the fragments were consulted.

Why this usually works
The generous reading is that language is redundant enough to survive damage. That is true, and
measurable: across 1,500 words, a single adjacent-key slip produces on average only 0.18 alternative
real words, and 86.0% of the time it produces none at all. Most typos are unambiguous simply because
nothing else could have been meant.
But this section is the honest counterweight to the rest of the paper, and it cuts both ways:
The tax in §3 is real and still paid. Reconstruction is not free. It costs the tokens, and it spends model
capacity on repair that could have gone to the actual task.
And it is silent. Nothing surfaces to say a word was rebuilt rather than read. When reconstruction
succeeds you never learn it happened; when it fails — when context is thin, or the domain is
unfamiliar, or the reader is a smaller model with less to draw on — it fails quietly and confidently,
which is the worst way for anything to fail.

10. Limits
One tokenizer was measured ( cl100k_base , 100,277 entries). Newer tokenizers have larger
vocabularies and close some of the language gap, though they do not eliminate it. The typo
experiments use synthetic single-character edits rather than a corpus of real human typos, which are
not uniformly distributed — real errors cluster on long and unfamiliar words, so the true cost in the
wild is probably higher than measured here, not lower. The translations are single sentences; a larger
corpus would tighten the language ratios.
Section 9 is the weakest empirically and should be read as such. The ambiguity figures are measured,
but the claim that context does the reconstructing is argued from mechanism and a single worked
example, not from a controlled experiment. Testing it properly would mean feeding identical typos to a
model with and without surrounding context and measuring recovery rate — a good next study, and
one this paper does not run.
None of these change the direction of any result.
